01 The Business What we sell, and how it makes money

Used-car buyers cannot see the one thing that decides the purchase.

1.1The problem

Problem statement. First-time used-car buyers in Australia commit five figures at the point of sale without the transparency, mechanical expertise or diagnostic tools needed to judge a vehicle’s true condition — so faults already recorded in the car’s computer stay invisible until they become the buyer’s repair bill.

Three gaps create it. Buyers cannot read the car’s electronics, so stored faults, pending faults and recently cleared codes never surface. They lack the expertise to price a symptom, so risk cannot be weighed against the asking price. And a professional inspection costs $250 to $400 and takes days to book, which is impractical across a shortlist of several cars — so most private buyers skip it and decide on a test drive and the seller’s word.

1.2Vehicle reliability over time

1.5Registration details

Before CarScan AI sells a single scanner, the founders will put the business on a legal footing. That means registering the business name with ASIC, obtaining an Australian Business Number through the Australian Business Register, and completing the tax registrations that follow from it — GST once we pass the turnover threshold, and PAYG withholding when we take on our first employee. CarScan AI is planned as a partnership, so the founders will also sign a written Partnership Agreement before trading begins, covering each partner’s ownership share, the capital each has contributed, how profits are divided, who is responsible for what, how decisions get made, what happens if a partner wants to leave, and how a disagreement between the two of us is settled.

None of this is paperwork for its own sake. The registrations are what let CarScan AI trade legally, invoice customers, open a business bank account and be taken seriously by the manufacturers and fulfilment partners who will not deal with an unregistered business at all. The Partnership Agreement protects the founders from each other: in a partnership each partner is personally liable for the debts of the business, so agreeing the terms in writing while everyone is optimistic costs a few hundred dollars, and not doing so can cost the business itself.

1.7Structure and ownership

CarScan AI operates under two structures in sequence, and the distinction matters because the two use different legal instruments. At launch it is a general partnership between the two founders, who hold percentage partnership interests — a partnership has no share capital, so no shares exist and none can be issued at this stage.

Before any outside capital is raised, the business incorporates as a proprietary limited company and the partnership interests convert to ordinary shares. Incorporation is what makes the raise possible at all: an investor cannot take an interest in a partnership without assuming unlimited personal liability for its debts, and an employee share option plan requires share capital to grant options over. Only from that point do the words “shares”, “ESOP” and “director” apply.

1.12Legal considerations and insurance

Selling a diagnostic product that people rely on carries obligations, and CarScan AI will take advice on them before launch rather than after the first complaint. The business will be bound by the Australian Consumer Law, which gives every buyer guarantees about the product that our own terms cannot exclude, and by privacy obligations covering customer details and the vehicle and diagnostic information the app collects. Before trading, CarScan AI will publish Terms and Conditions and a Privacy Policy that state plainly what data is stored, where it is stored, how long it is kept and what it is used for, and will hold product liability, professional indemnity and public liability insurance.

The specific risk being managed is that customers use our results to make a decision worth tens of thousands of dollars. If a scan were incomplete or a repair estimate wrong, a buyer could argue that CarScan AI contributed to a financial loss. That is why every result the app produces says exactly what it is — diagnostic guidance read from the car’s own computer, not a substitute for a professional mechanical inspection — and why the app never overstates what the AI can tell you. Clear terms, an honest disclaimer on every scan, responsible claims and insurance behind all of it protect the customer first and the business second.

The Finance
What we need, what we earn, what it is worth

Scanners pay the bills now, reports pay them later.

The money side is simple: selling scanners pays the bills early on, and selling reports pays them later. Each scanner sells for well over what it costs us to make and ship, so every one we sell puts money in rather than taking it out. The app stays free, because it is what makes the scanner worth buying in the first place.

4.1What we make on each scanner
Where every $89 goes
Each bar is one scanner sold. Just over a third of the price covers making and shipping it, and $9 goes on card and postage fees. A quarter goes on finding the customer. What is left over — $24 — is ours.
The point where the business pays for itself
Some costs stay the same no matter how many we sell, so every extra scanner closes the gap. The lines cross at three thousand units — after that, the business is funding itself.

Each scanner sells from $89 and costs us $28 to $34 to make, ship and land in Australia, including GST. That leaves us a little over sixty cents in every dollar before we spend anything on finding customers. The app costs nothing to hand out, so an extra user costs us nothing once they own a scanner. We expect to cover all our costs at three thousand scanners sold.

4.4What we expect to earn and spend

The three-year forecast shows a planned loss of $138,000 in the first year while we build up customers, a small profit in the second, and $658,000 before tax in the third. What we keep from each sale rises from 63 to 67 cents in the dollar, because bigger batches cost less per scanner and reports cost almost nothing to produce.

Year one loses money on purpose while we build up customers. We turn a profit in year two, and the gap widens as report sales arrive.

4.5What the business is worth

What the business owns stays simple: cash in the bank, scanners in stock and a small amount of equipment. What it owes is money due to suppliers. The difference — what the business is actually worth — grows from $372,000 at the end of year one to $1.11 million by year three, when the profits finally outweigh the first-year loss.
